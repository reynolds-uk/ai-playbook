# Where the revenue is

Most AI plans are cost plans. This page is for the other side: finding revenue in work you already do, and checking that the way you charge lets you keep what AI saves. It goes with [Pay for the outcome](https://ai.valuecreator.io/read/pay-for-the-outcome), where the reasoning and three examples are written up.

**Small team:** an hour with whoever owns your biggest customer relationships. **Large business:** an afternoon per business line, with the people who price the work in the room.

## In plain terms

Savings from AI tend to pass to customers as competitors buy the same tools. They stay with you longer when you charge for the outcome rather than the activity. The larger prize is usually something new to sell, and in a services or information business it's often hiding in work you already do: data nobody has joined up, figures nobody explains, and records of your own work.

## Four questions

Answer each one with specifics: a customer, a dataset, a report, a price.

1. **What do we see, across our customers or suppliers, that none of them can see on their own?** Look for data you hold from several sources that has never been joined up, because nobody's job was to join it.
2. **What do we produce as numbers that someone would pay to have explained?** Language models make fluent commentary easy. What's scarce is knowing which change matters and what a customer should do about it.
3. **What do we already record about our own work that would show us what to automate, and when?** A record of how work actually flows is worth more than any single automation. It lets you choose the moment, and leave some decisions to a future owner.
4. **What do customers value that we currently charge for by the hour, the search or the report?** As AI takes the cost of producing it down, a price per unit follows it down.

Most of the time the first question has the most valuable answer, and the fourth decides whether you keep any of it.

## The pricing check

One row per thing you sell. Be honest in the last column.

| What we sell | How we charge now (hour, unit, subscription, outcome) | What it's worth to the customer when it goes right | What it costs them when it goes wrong | Will AI make it cheaper to produce? | Could we charge for the outcome? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| | | | | | |

Anything charged per hour or per unit, that AI will make cheaper to produce, is where the saving will leave first.

## What to do next

- Pick one answer to question 1 or 2 and describe the product in a paragraph: who buys it, what they'd do differently, and what they'd pay.
- Pick one line from the pricing check. Ask two customers what the result is worth to them, and put one proposal to them on that basis.
- Keep the new revenue in its own column, apart from savings, in [the two columns](tools/04-two-columns/).

## Small team or large business

**Small team:** the four questions are usually answered by one or two people who know the customers well. Do it before you set this year's prices.

**Large business:** the answers to question 1 often sit across business lines, and nobody owns them. Name one person to own each idea you decide to test, with a stop point written down, as in [stages and gates](tools/03-stages-and-gates/).
